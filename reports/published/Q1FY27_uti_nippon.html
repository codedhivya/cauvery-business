📐 AMC Metrics 101 — What the Numbers Actually Mean
QAAUM vs Closing AUM vs Total/Group AUM ▾ click to expand
QAAUM (Quarterly Average AUM) is the SEBI-mandated average of daily (or weekly) net assets over the quarter, used specifically for an AMC's mutual fund schemes — it's the number that actually drives management-fee revenue, since fees are charged on average assets, not a single snapshot. Closing AUM is simply the MF assets on the last day of the period — more volatile, easily swung by one big market move or redemption near quarter-end. Total/Group AUM is the broadest figure and can include non-MF businesses entirely — pension/NPS assets, PMS, AIFs, offshore advisory mandates — which don't earn MF-style fees and can dwarf the MF book in size. Always check which AUM a headline number refers to before comparing two companies — as this dashboard's UTI AMC figures (₹3.93 lakh Cr MF QAAUM vs ₹20.57 lakh Cr Total Group AUM) illustrate.
Revenue Yield (bps) ▾ click to expand
The management fee an AMC earns, expressed in basis points (bps, 1 bps = 0.01%) of QAAUM. Equity schemes typically carry the highest yield (SEBI caps are higher for equity than debt), so an AMC's blended yield rises or falls mainly with its equity mix, not just fee-rate changes. A rising yield can reflect either a genuinely richer asset mix or one-off effects (e.g., a low-yield ETF category growing fast can actually pull blended yield down even as absolute revenue rises) — always check the mix commentary alongside the yield number.
Core PAT vs Consolidated PAT ▾ click to expand
Consolidated PAT includes everything — the core MF business, subsidiaries (pension, PMS, international, AIF), and "other income" from the AMC's own treasury/investment book (mark-to-market gains, dividends on seed capital, etc.). Core PAT (or "core operating profit," terminology varies by company) strips out subsidiary contributions and one-off/treasury effects to isolate how the actual fund-management business performed. The two can diverge sharply in a single quarter — as this dashboard shows for UTI AMC (Q1 FY27 consolidated PAT +24% YoY, but core PAT −6% YoY) — which is why CB Research always checks both before calling an earnings "beat" high- or low-quality.
Equity vs Non-Equity Mix ▾ click to expand
The split of an AMC's QAAUM between equity-oriented schemes (equity, equity-hybrid, ELSS) and non-equity (debt, liquid, index/ETF, FoF). Because equity schemes earn meaningfully higher fees, a higher equity mix generally means higher blended yield and margin — one reason AMCs with an equity-heavy book (like UTI AMC's 70:30 mix within its own MF book) can look structurally more profitable per rupee of AUM than more debt-heavy peers, all else equal.
Stock Market Share vs Flow Market Share ▾ click to expand
Stock market share is an AMC's share of total industry AUM at a point in time — a slow-moving, backward-looking measure. Flow market share is its share of net new money coming into the industry each period — a leading indicator. If flow share consistently exceeds stock share, an AMC is gaining ground; if flow share is below stock share (as UTI AMC's own CFO acknowledged in the Q1 FY27 call), the AMC is losing relative ground even if its absolute AUM is still growing. This is the metric to watch for early signs of a turnaround (or a continued slide) before it shows up in the slower-moving stock-share number.
B-30 Towns & Folios ▾ click to expand
B-30 refers to locations "Beyond the Top 30" Indian cities by MF AUM — SEBI has historically incentivised AMCs to expand into these under-penetrated markets, so B-30 share is a proxy for how far an AMC's distribution reach extends beyond metro India. A folio is an individual investment account (one investor can hold several folios across schemes), so "folio count" is a rough, imperfect proxy for retail reach — better read alongside "unique investors," which counts individuals rather than accounts.

📜 Company Backgrounder
UTI AMC — A Short History
UTI traces back to the Unit Trust of India, set up by an Act of Parliament in 1963 as India's first mutual fund, and for decades the dominant (often only) player in Indian retail investing. Its flagship US-64 scheme became a household name. In 2001–02, US-64 faced a redemption/valuation crisis, leading to a government-backed restructuring in 2002–03 that split the entity: a government-guaranteed "Specified Undertaking" (SUUTI, wound down over subsequent years) and a new, SEBI-regulated UTI Asset Management Company, sponsored jointly by SBI, LIC, Bank of Baroda and Punjab National Bank. In 2010, T. Rowe Price bought into the company (see Investor Spotlight below). UTI AMC listed on the NSE/BSE in October 2020. It remains India's oldest mutual fund brand, with a wide pension (NPS), international, and alternates business alongside its core MF franchise, and is currently mid-turnaround under a new CEO (Vetri Subramaniam, since Feb 2026) pursuing a "Mission 2031" plan to double AUM.
Nippon Life India AMC — A Short History
The business began in 1995 as Reliance Mutual Fund, part of the Reliance/Anil Dhirubhai Ambani Group, and grew into one of India's largest private-sector AMCs through the 2000s. Japan's Nippon Life Insurance Company first took a stake in 2011–2012 as a strategic joint-venture partner (the fund house was renamed "Reliance Nippon Life Asset Management"), and progressively increased its holding over the following years as Reliance Capital's broader financial-services group ran into stress. In 2019, Nippon Life acquired Reliance Capital's remaining stake, becoming the clear majority promoter, and the company was renamed Nippon Life India Asset Management (NAM India) in 2020. It had listed on the NSE/BSE back in November 2017 (as Reliance Nippon Life AMC, pre-renaming). Today it is one of India's largest AMCs, under long-tenured CEO Sundeep Sikka, known for its scale in ETFs, digital distribution and a broad retail/HNI/corporate investor base.
🤝 Investor Spotlight
🇺🇸 T. Rowe Price — UTI AMC's largest shareholder. T. Rowe Price is a large American investment management firm headquartered in Baltimore, Maryland, and one of the biggest publicly-listed asset managers in the US — running mutual funds, retirement/401(k) products and institutional money, best known for active equity and fixed-income management plus target-date retirement funds.

Its UTI AMC connection dates back to January 2010: T. Rowe Price Group Inc., through its subsidiary T. Rowe Price Global Investment Services (a UK entity), bought a 26% stake in UTI Asset Management Company and UTI Trustee Company for about ₹650 crore (~$142 million). The stake was bought from the four original sponsors — SBI, LIC, Bank of Baroda and Punjab National Bank — who each sold down 6.5%, leaving them at 18.5% apiece at the time.

Over the years T. Rowe Price's holding has drifted somewhat (various sources put it anywhere from ~23% to ~26% currently), but it has remained UTI AMC's single largest shareholder since that 2010 deal. The four PSU sponsors don't count as "promoters" under SEBI rules, since they each already run their own separate AMC businesses and SEBI bars an entity from being classified as promoter of more than one AMC — which is why UTI AMC is often described as having "no identifiable promoter." UTI AMCUTI AMC's consolidated PAT grew 24.1% YoY to ₹293.9 Cr, but the company's own disclosed "core PAT" fell 6% YoY to ₹129 Cr. How should an investor reconcile these two very different-looking numbers?
A. The consolidated PAT growth is the real story — ignore core PAT, it's just an internal management metric.
B. Consolidated PAT includes subsidiary (pension, international, PMS/AIF) and treasury/investment gains that grew much faster than the core standalone MF-management business, which itself is still under pressure from market-share losses.
C. It signals an accounting error between standalone and consolidated filings.
CB's view: Answer B. Standalone PAT (the core AMC entity) grew just 0.93% YoY to ₹218 Cr, essentially flat, while consolidated PAT of ₹293.9 Cr benefited from stronger subsidiary performance (pension, international, PMS/AIF) and the sharp sequential swing from Q4 FY26's consolidated loss. The company's own "core PAT" metric of ₹129 Cr (down 6% YoY) is arguably the cleanest read on the actual fund-management business's earnings power, stripping out these subsidiary and one-off effects — and it's still declining even as the headline consolidated number looks like a strong beat. Nippon Life India AMCNAM India trades at roughly 48x trailing P/E — nearly double UTI AMC's ~27x — while both companies operate in the same industry with similar structural tailwinds (rising Indian retail participation in mutual funds). What justifies this gap?
A. It's mostly noise/sentiment — the two businesses are fundamentally similar.
B. NAM India is gaining market share (9.04%, a 7-year high) with a core-operating-profit-led earnings beat, while UTI AMC is losing market share (4.72%, down from 5.00%) with a core PAT that's actually shrinking — the market is pricing the demonstrated growth trajectory, not just current-quarter size.
C. NAM India's promoter (Nippon Life, Japan) directly subsidizes its share price.
CB's view: Answer B. Valuation multiples in asset management are heavily driven by AUM/market-share trajectory, since fee revenue scales almost directly with AUM. NAM India's Q1 FY27 showed the industry's largest market-share gain, a core operating profit growing faster than even its strong revenue growth (31% vs 26% YoY), and leadership in the fast-growing ETF category. UTI AMC, by contrast, is losing share in its highest-margin active-equity segment and its core (ex-subsidiary) profitability is shrinking. The ~48x vs ~27x gap is large, but it is explainable by a genuine and currently-diverging growth/quality differential — not simply promoter identity or sentiment. Whether NAM India's premium is fully "earned" going forward, or UTI AMC's discount is "too cheap," are separate judgment calls each investor must make. Not investment advice.

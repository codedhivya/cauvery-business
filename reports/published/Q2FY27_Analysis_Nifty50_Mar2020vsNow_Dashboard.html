Is this close to the bottom?

Not a confirmed bottom, but not expensive either. The sell-off is much shallower than 2020 (−15% against about −39%), valuations are fair to slightly cheap, and earnings are still growing. But the macro set-up is the opposite of 2020: oil, yields and rate-hike risk are rising, where in 2020 they were falling. And there has been no panic yet (VIX is only 14).

If it falls further, is it the right time to buy? Only in stages. A falling market on its own is not the signal. Deeper levels (around 21,100, 19,800, 18,500) make valuation progressively more attractive, but buying everything at once assumes you can time the low. See the Buy Plan tab to test staged buying with your own numbers.

Two things that make 2026 different from 2020

Domestic money: demat accounts rose from about 4 crore to 23.8 crore, and record SIPs helped absorb about ₹3 lakh crore of foreign selling, so the fall is 15%, not 39%. IPO window: a record September for IPOs shows issuers can still raise money. Cautions: accounts are not investors, the cushion depends on SIPs continuing, and about 60% of IPO money is promoters selling. See the Domestic Money & IPOs tab.

Reading the multiples.
  • On trailing P/E (19.4) the index is at its long-run average of about 20, so it is not cheap on that measure.
  • On a 12-month forward basis (16.9) it is about 12% below the 15-year average of 19.1 and 17% below the 10-year average of 20.3. That is mildly cheap, provided earnings are delivered.
  • In 2020 the trailing P/E of about 18 looked fair, but the true low on forward earnings was about 13–15. Today's forward multiple is about 20% higher than that 2020 low. The valuation cushion is smaller than in 2020.

Why oil and yields matter more now than in 2020

Oil. India imports more than 85% of its crude, and about half of that passed through the Strait of Hormuz when the conflict began. In 2020 Brent collapsed to about $27–35, which helped India's import bill and inflation. Today it is back near $100.

Diesel squeeze: the US export-ban threat and Europe's reserves

New in 2026, and nothing like March 2020. The US is the world's largest diesel exporter. President Trump has said he is "very seriously" considering a ban on diesel exports to cut US pump prices before the November midterms, and the US has reportedly told Europe that continued exports depend on Europe releasing its strategic diesel stocks. No ban has been imposed. On Friday 2 Oct the G7 agreed to release 100 million barrels over four months, with a front-loaded release in the first 20 days, coordinated through the International Energy Agency. Trump said Europe had agreed to a "massive" release.

What it means for India and the Nifty (my analysis).
  • Relief case: if the G7 release works and no ban comes, diesel and crude prices can ease, which helps India's import bill, inflation, the rupee and RBI. The first test is Monday 5 Oct, because Indian markets were shut on Friday 2 Oct.
  • Risk case: a ban, or a delayed release, would send global diesel prices higher and Europe would compete with Latin America and West Africa for cargoes. India would feel it through freight and fuel inflation.
  • Winners and losers: export-oriented refiners can earn higher diesel margins; oil marketing companies and transport-heavy sectors are squeezed. Indian diesel exports to Europe fell from about 163,000 to 50,000 b/d (a lower-grade data point), so India's direct gain is smaller than it once would have been.
  • Timing: a release spread over four months is a bridge, not a fix. It does not reopen Hormuz.

Oil sensitivity (illustrative)

Rule of thumb used: each sustained $10/bbl rise widens India's current account deficit by about 0.5% of GDP and trims Asian growth by 0.2–0.3 percentage points. Baseline: $65 before the war, deficit about 1.2% of GDP. This is a simple linear sketch, not a forecast.

Did fixed income lose money in the last 5 years?

Short answer: mostly no in rupee terms, but it earned very little after inflation, and long bonds lost money along the way. Gilt (government bond) funds have returned about 5.7% a year over 5 years, which is positive but only about 1 point above inflation of roughly 4–5%. Bond prices fell whenever yields rose (2022, and again this year as the 10-year went from about 6.3% in Oct 2021 to 7.21%), so investors in long-duration funds saw painful stretches, including a weak last 12 months (gilt funds 1-year returns of about 2–3%). In the same 5 years the Nifty also did little: about 4.9% a year in price, about 6% with dividends.

Is money really moving from fixed income to equities?

The data does not show a wholesale switch. Debt mutual funds took in about ₹1.88 lakh crore in July 2026, then saw a ₹8,127 crore outflow in August (mostly corporates pulling out of liquid funds for quarter-end tax payments). Equity funds received ₹29,329 crore in August, but that is mainly steady SIP money (a record ₹32,297 crore a month), not people selling debt to buy equity. Large-cap funds actually had net outflows (₹1,147 crore) and balanced-advantage funds had outflows in July. Foreign investors, meanwhile, are selling Indian equities.

Bond versus equity calculator

Simplified: equity return = EPS growth plus change in P/E (starting at 19.36) plus a 1.22% dividend yield; bond return assumes you hold to maturity at today's yield. Equity gains are taxed at 12.5% and bond interest at your slab. Equity returns are never smooth; bonds can lose money before maturity.

Balanced reading. You are right that the yield is good, and today's 7.21% is a better starting point than five years ago. It would be wrong to say equity is clearly better. Equity wins only if earnings grow at least about 6% a year with no fall in P/E (the calculator shows the exact figure). A sensible approach is by time horizon: money needed within 3 years stays in short-duration bonds or deposits; money for 5 years or more can be moved into equity in stages, since earnings growth, not the dividend, drives the return.

Two big differences from 2020

1. A much bigger domestic investor base. Demat accounts have grown from about 4 crore in March 2020 to about 23.8 crore in August 2026, nearly six times. Money arrives every month through SIPs (a record ₹32,297 crore in August) and through direct investing. That is why foreign selling of about ₹3 lakh crore in 2026 has been absorbed, and the Nifty is down 15% instead of 39%.

2. A wide-open primary market. Active retail participation in the secondary market has fed the IPO market. September 2026 was the busiest month for mainboard IPOs in about 30 years (34 issues, about ₹39,380 crore), even while the Nifty was falling. Issuers can raise money, which supports capital formation and jobs.

A fair reading

What is right. The domestic cushion is real. Foreigners sold a record amount, domestic institutions bought more than that, and the index fell far less than in 2020. This is a genuine structural difference and it is the main reason this sell-off looks different.
What to keep in mind.
  • The market has fallen. It is down 15% with eight straight weekly falls and a drop of about 11% in March alone. The cushion softens falls; it does not stop them.
  • Other reasons also explain the shallower fall. The 2020 crash was a sudden global liquidity freeze (VIX 86) that started from a very high P/E of about 28 in January 2020. Earnings are growing now.
  • Accounts are not investors. Only about 13 crore unique investors and about 4.6 crore active clients sit behind 23.8 crore accounts.
  • The cushion depends on SIPs. If market losses make people stop SIPs, the support weakens. Watch the monthly SIP data and stoppage ratio. In 2020 the retail base had not yet been tested through a long fall.
  • IPOs: a good window, with a caution. About 60% of IPO money is offer for sale, so existing holders are selling. A record IPO month can also be a sign of froth, and part of September's rush came from a regulatory deadline.
  • Foreigners still matter. They hold about 15% of Indian equities, and their selling weakens the rupee and sets the tone for large-cap prices.
Effect on the verdict. A stronger domestic base makes a 2020-style 39% collapse less likely. In the Buy Plan tab, lowering the bear-case probability from 25% to 20% (base 55%, bull 25%) lifts the probability-weighted value from about 23,230 to about 23,530, roughly +5%. That still points to staged buying rather than all-in, because oil, yields and the rupee are unchanged.

The index's rule-book

Methodology. The big switch from full to free-float market capitalisation happened in June 2009, so it is not a change between 2020 and now. Since then the refinements are about eligibility and concentration: stocks must be in the F&O segment, trade at an impact cost of 0.50% or less, and a newcomer must have at least 1.5 times the free-float value of the smallest member. Weights are capped (the current rulebook cites a 15% single-stock cap) and the index is rebalanced twice a year. Check NSE's methodology document for the exact current rules.
Why it matters: the Nifty of 2026 is a different basket from the Nifty of 2020. Comparing levels or P/E across the two is useful but never exact.

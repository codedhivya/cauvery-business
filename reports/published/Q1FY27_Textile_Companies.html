⚠️ Q1 FY27 Context: All five companies declared results in the first two weeks of August 2026, and all five beat their Q1 FY26 base on both revenue and profit — a sharp turnaround from the tariff-hit FY26. US tariff: the punitive IEEPA reciprocal tariff regime was struck down by the US Supreme Court (20 Feb 2026); a flat 10% Section 122 tariff applied through 24 Jul 2026, after which it was replaced by a two-tier Section 301 "forced-labour" tariff — India sits in the lower 10% tier alongside Bangladesh and Indonesia, while Vietnam and China sit in the higher 12.5% tier. Apparel's high MFN base (16.5–17% on cotton knits/wovens) means landed US tariffs on Indian garments are still meaningfully higher than pre-2025 levels, but India now has tariff parity with its nearest rivals rather than a punitive gap — Gokaldas management explicitly cited this, and Pearl Global's multi-country platform (India, Bangladesh, Vietnam, Indonesia, Guatemala) lets it route orders to whichever base is most tariff-efficient. UK CETA: signed 24 Jul 2025, textiles get zero-duty access from day one once the treaty is ratified; both parliaments are still completing domestic ratification as of this report, with entry into force expected in H2 CY2026 — SP Apparels (via its UK subsidiary Zenith/SPUK) is the clearest direct beneficiary, with Pearl Global's management also flagging the UK FTA and a proposed India-EU deal as tailwinds for its diversified platform.
🇺🇸 US Tariff Regime — Section 301, 10% Tier Effective 24 Jul 2026
India moved from the flat 10% Section 122 surcharge (Feb–Jul 2026) to the two-tier Section 301 forced-labour tariff, landing in the lower 10% tier with Bangladesh and Indonesia — versus 12.5% for Vietnam and China. Apparel's high MFN base keeps total landed duty elevated, but the competitive gap has closed.
▲ Gokaldas · KPR · Nitin Spinners (indirect via customers)
🇬🇧 India-UK CETA — Ratification Underway Signed 24 Jul 2025
Textiles & apparel get zero-duty UK access from day one of entry into force. Both parliaments are finishing domestic ratification; industry expectation is H2 CY2026. SP Apparels' UK arm (Zenith/SPUK) is the direct beneficiary; management there is already citing new UK/Europe customer interest.
Max beneficiary: SP Apparels · Also: Gokaldas, KPR (secondary)

Business Analysis — Q1 FY27
KPR Mill — Vertically Integrated Compounder

KPR Mill's business model — spinning yarn, weaving/knitting fabric, and stitching garments all in-house, with sugar and ethanol as a diversifying by-product business — gives it cost control that few peers can match, and shows up as the sector's best-in-class EBITDA margins historically. Its export book skews heavily to the EU, which kept it largely insulated from the US tariff shock that dominated FY26. Q1 FY27's growth was helped as much by the sugar segment as by textiles, which is worth remembering when comparing its margin to pure-play textile peers. With ₹772 Cr of net cash and no debt, capital allocation risk is low; the open question for investors is less about the business and more about whether the stock's already-premium valuation leaves room for further re-rating (a point MOFSL explicitly flagged post-results).

5 Key Takeaways
  • Best all-round quarter in the set: +9.3% revenue, +21.5% consol. PAT, +27.4% standalone PAT — broad-based, not driven by one line item.
  • Not a pure textile story: growth was aided by the sugar segment as much as textiles — factor this in before comparing its margin to garmenting-only peers.
  • Strongest balance sheet by far: ₹772 Cr net cash, debt-free — the only company in this set funding its FY27-28 capacity expansion without external debt.
  • Lowest US tariff exposure: EU-tilted export mix means it is largely a bystander to the US Section 301 tariff story driving Gokaldas and, indirectly, Nitin Spinners.
  • Valuation is the real debate, not the business: stock rallied 6.2% post-results and already trades at a premium multiple; MOFSL flagged limited near-term upside despite the strong print.
SP Apparels — UK-Anchored Kids-Wear Specialist

SP Apparels is built around kids' and infant wear manufacturing (Avinashi standalone + Young Brand Apparel) with a UK-facing subsidiary (SPUK, trading as Zenith Clothing) that gives it a structural edge once the India-UK CETA is ratified — textiles get zero-duty UK access from day one. Q1 FY27 showed the company prioritising margin over volume: revenue was flat but EBITDA margin expanded 220 bps as cost discipline and product mix improved. Sri Lanka is the other growth lever — factories there are approaching full utilisation and are expected to add ₹150-200 Cr of FY27 revenue, diversifying the manufacturing base beyond India. Management's own commentary is cautious near-term (normalisation only from Q2 FY27, fuller recovery from Q3) but constructive on FY27-28, targeting ₹2,000 Cr and then ₹2,500 Cr of consolidated revenue.

5 Key Takeaways
  • Margin-over-volume quarter: revenue essentially flat (-0.6%) but EBITDA margin expanded 220 bps to 15.3% and PAT grew 20.4% — profit quality improved even without top-line growth.
  • Clearest direct UK CETA beneficiary in this set: owned UK subsidiary SPUK/Zenith Clothing means it can capture zero-duty access immediately once the treaty is ratified, rather than building buyer relationships from scratch.
  • Sri Lanka still ramping, not yet at full contribution: one factory running at 85-90% utilisation, targeted at ₹150-200 Cr FY27 revenue — a real but partly future lever.
  • Management itself frames this as a transition period: normalisation expected only from Q2 FY27, fuller recovery from Q3 FY27 — so near-term numbers likely understate the FY27-28 target run-rate.
  • Corporate actions signal confidence, not a fundamentals shift: ₹3/share dividend plus a 1:5 stock split announced alongside results — mechanically neutral for value, but a board-level positive signal.
Nitin Spinners — Cyclical Yarn Play With a Capacity Bet

Nitin Spinners is a Bhilwara-based, largely export-oriented (62% of FY26 revenue) cotton and melange yarn spinner that has been expanding downstream into knitted fabric and adding captive renewable power to control input costs. Its business is inherently more cyclical than the branded/garmenting names in this set — its economics are driven by the spread between cotton input cost and yarn realisation, which is exactly what recovered sharply in Q1 FY27 (EBITDA margin +376 bps). The company is now committing ₹1,350 Cr of largely debt-funded capex toward a ₹3,800 Cr FY27 revenue target — a credible plan given the CARE Ratings upgrade to A+ (Stable), but one that adds leverage and execution risk exactly as the stock has already re-rated sharply (+104% from its January 2026 low).

5 Key Takeaways
  • Sharpest turnaround in the sector: PAT +83.6% YoY and +31.2% QoQ — both directions confirm a genuine operating recovery, not a low-base artefact.
  • Margin expansion is the real driver, not just volume: EBITDA margin jumped 376 bps to 17.78%; revenue itself grew a more modest 10.3%.
  • Management directly linked the recovery to easing US tariff pressure — a reminder that even yarn spinners, one step removed from finished garments, feel trade-policy shifts through customer demand.
  • ₹1,350 Cr debt-funded capex is the key FY28 swing factor: credible given the CARE A+ upgrade, but leverage will rise before the new capacity contributes revenue.
  • Valuation has already re-rated sharply: stock up 104% from its January 2026 low, and some analysts have shifted to "Hold" — the easy re-rating may be behind it.
Gokaldas Exports — Scale Grower With a Margin Question

Gokaldas is one of India's largest apparel exporters, running 30+ production units across India, Kenya and Ethiopia and shipping to 50+ countries. Its dual-geography model — Indian manufacturing plus AGOA-eligible African operations — is a genuine structural hedge against US tariff swings that most India-only peers lack, and it showed up clearly this quarter: Africa revenue grew 45% while overall Indian apparel exports fell 12%. The company also explicitly credited the shift to a "tariff parity" US regime for its resilience. The catch is margin: EBITDA margin held roughly flat rather than expanding even as revenue surged 21%, reflecting wage inflation and freight cost pressure, and the pending BTPL (BRFL Textiles) merger adds near-term integration complexity. The market's -4.75% results-day reaction suggests investors are watching margin trajectory as closely as growth.

5 Key Takeaways
  • Fastest revenue growth in the sector: +21% total income, with India business growing 16% despite a 12% sector-wide decline in Indian apparel exports.
  • Africa/AGOA is now doing the heavy lifting: Africa revenue +45% YoY on AGOA's renewal (through Dec 2026) — a geographic hedge most India-only peers don't have.
  • Growth isn't converting into proportional profit: EBITDA margin held flat at 11.8% and consol. PAT grew just 6.8%, well behind the 21% revenue growth — wages and freight are the drag.
  • Standalone (India) is outperforming the consolidated group: standalone PAT +43% vs consolidated +6.8%, pointing to BTPL/Africa as the relative weak spots pulling down the group number.
  • Two policy-dependent supports need periodic renewal: RoSCTL export incentives expire Sept 2026 and AGOA itself is a renewable US law — both are watch-items into H2 FY27, alongside the BTPL merger's targeted Q3 FY27 completion.
Pearl Global Industries — India's Largest Listed Garment Exporter, Multi-Country by Design

Pearl Global's business model is fundamentally different from the other four names in this set: rather than concentrating manufacturing in one country, it runs production across India, Bangladesh, Vietnam, Indonesia and Guatemala, letting it route orders to whichever base offers the best cost, capacity or tariff position at any given time. Management explicitly called Q1 FY27 the company's best quarter on record, with broad-based growth across all locations, record shipment volumes (20.8 million pieces, +21% YoY), and a record 10.7% EBITDA margin. The trade-off for this diversification is margin level — at 10.7%, its EBITDA margin is the thinnest of the five companies here, reflecting a sourcing/manufacturing-platform model rather than a vertically integrated or branded one. A Bangladesh capacity expansion due online in September 2026 will lift group capacity to roughly 108 million garments annually, and management has explicitly flagged both the India-UK FTA and a proposed India-EU trade agreement as future tailwinds for its India-anchored capacity.

5 Key Takeaways
  • Best quarter in company history: revenue +24.5%, adjusted EBITDA +44.1%, PAT +51.4% — and management describes the growth as broad-based across all five manufacturing geographies, not concentrated in one.
  • Widest built-in tariff hedge in this set: a five-country manufacturing footprint (India, Bangladesh, Vietnam, Indonesia, Guatemala) means no single country's trade policy can swing the whole business the way it can for India-only exporters.
  • Margin is a genuine record but still the thinnest here: 10.7% EBITDA margin, up 140 bps YoY, is Pearl Global's best-ever — but structurally lower than the branded/vertically-integrated names in this set, a feature of the sourcing-platform model.
  • Capacity is expanding into FY27-28: a Bangladesh addition (+~7 mn garments/year, online Sept 2026) takes group capacity to ~108 mn garments annually — a concrete, near-term growth lever rather than a longer-dated one.
  • Management is positioning for both the UK and EU tracks: explicitly cited the India-UK FTA and a proposed India-EU deal as tailwinds — a second and third trade-policy catalyst beyond the US tariff story that dominates this quarter's sector narrative.

🎓 School — Understanding This Quarter
Why did the US tariff on Indian textiles change three times in a year?
In August 2025, the US applied a 50% combined tariff on Indian goods (25% "reciprocal" tariff + a 25% penalty tied to India's purchase of Russian crude oil). In February 2026, a bilateral interim trade deal cut the reciprocal rate to 18% and removed the Russia-linked penalty. Then, on 20 February 2026, the US Supreme Court struck down the legal basis (IEEPA) for the reciprocal tariff entirely — that gap was filled by a flat 10% "Section 122" tariff, which itself expired after its 150-day statutory limit on 24 July 2026 and was replaced the same day by a two-tier "Section 301" forced-labour tariff, where India landed in the lower 10% tier. Each of these steps changed the competitive gap between India and rivals like Bangladesh and Vietnam — which is why "the tariff" isn't a single fixed number this year.
This is educational background, not a forecast of where the rate goes next — trade policy in this cycle has changed roughly every 2-5 months.
What does "tariff parity" actually mean for a company like Gokaldas?
It doesn't mean tariffs are low — apparel's base US duty (MFN rate) is already high, 16.5-17% on cotton knits/wovens even before any surcharge. "Parity" means India's total landed tariff is now similar to its nearest rivals (Bangladesh, Indonesia) rather than meaningfully higher, as it was during the 50% tariff period in late 2025. A US buyer choosing between an Indian and a Bangladeshi supplier is no longer paying a large India-specific penalty — so orders that were diverted away in 2025 have room to come back on cost grounds alone.
Why does the India-UK CETA matter even though it isn't in force yet?
Trade agreements are usually priced in stages by markets and by companies: first the signing (July 2025, sentiment/optionality), then ratification progress (ongoing through 2026), then actual entry into force (zero-duty access begins), and finally the multi-year ramp as buyers actually shift orders. SP Apparels is positioning now — new customer conversations, Sri Lanka capacity — specifically so it can capture volume the moment the treaty is ratified, rather than starting from zero. That's why "not yet in force" doesn't mean "not yet relevant."
Why did Nitin Spinners' profit jump 83.6% when revenue only grew 10.3%?
Nitin Spinners is a yarn spinner — its core economics are the spread between cotton input cost and yarn selling price, not just volume. When that spread widens (as it did this quarter, with EBITDA margin rising from 14.02% to 17.78%), profit grows much faster than revenue because most operating costs are relatively fixed. This is called operating leverage, and it's a defining feature of commodity-adjacent manufacturers like spinners — profits are far more volatile than revenue in both directions, which is also why the stock has been more volatile than the branded-apparel names in this set.
Why did Gokaldas Exports' stock fall on a quarter with 21% revenue growth?
Markets tend to price the quality of growth, not just its size. Gokaldas grew revenue 21% but EBITDA margin stayed roughly flat (11.8%) rather than expanding, meaning the extra revenue didn't convert into proportionally extra profit — consolidated PAT grew only 6.8%. When a stock has run up in anticipation of a strong quarter, a "good but not great on margins" result can trigger a sell-off even with a strong headline number, because the market was pricing in margin expansion that didn't show up.
What is AGOA, and why does it matter for Gokaldas but not the others here?
AGOA (African Growth and Opportunity Act) is a US law giving eligible African countries — including Kenya and Ethiopia, where Gokaldas has manufacturing — duty-free access to the US market. It needs periodic US Congressional renewal, and uncertainty around that renewal hurt Gokaldas's Africa business for much of FY26 (revenue there fell 19%) before it was restored (through December 2026), after which Africa revenue grew 45% in a single quarter. None of the other three companies in this set has comparable AGOA-eligible manufacturing, which is why this is a Gokaldas-specific risk/opportunity to track.

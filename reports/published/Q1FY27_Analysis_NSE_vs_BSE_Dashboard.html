Bottom line. NSE is the stronger, deeper, more profitable franchise and the only one with a near-monopoly moat (93% of cash equities, 100% of stock derivatives, #1 globally by derivatives contracts). BSE is the faster-growing, cheaper-to-trade challenger that has already captured the easy share gains. At today's prices both are expensive against global exchanges (21-34x P/E), both live on options volumes, and both face the same regulator. My verdict: NSE is the better long-term core holding, but only at a price that gives a margin of safety; BSE is a higher-beta satellite. Section "3-5 Year View" shows the arithmetic.

Two one-offs distort FY26: a ₹1,432 cr provision for the SEBI colocation and dark-fibre settlement (reduces EBITDA) and a ₹1,201 cr pre-tax gain on the partial NSDL stake sale (increases PAT). Excluding the NSDL gain (about ₹1,050-1,200 cr after tax), the P/E at the upper band is roughly 48x, not 43x. Adding back the provision as well brings it back to about 42x. Either way NSE is priced well above global peers.

Reading the BSE numbers. Growth is exceptional but the trajectory has cooled: brokers cut FY27-FY29 EPS by 5-15% in August on the closing-auction session, higher STT and tighter RBI bank-guarantee norms. BSE options volumes fell about 12% in August versus about 14% for NSE.

Simple analogy. An exchange is like the Koyambedu wholesale market. Traders (brokers) rent stalls and pay a small fee on every deal. The market owner does not sell vegetables, so it takes no price risk; it earns on volume. NSE is the giant central market where 93% of the buying and selling happens. BSE is the older market next door, now offering near-zero fees on some counters to pull traders across.

What the comparison says. NSE wins on volume (51% of the world's equity-derivative contracts), margin and cost efficiency, and it is growing in a young, under-penetrated market. But global exchanges earn a mix of data, clearing, technology and multi-asset trading fees and have grown faster recently. NSE is asking investors to pay about 1.6-2x their P/E and 2x their EV/EBITDA while its revenue grew slower. The premium is justified only if India's capital-market financialisation delivers 15%+ earnings growth for many years.

Is it good for a 3 to 5 year holding?

NSE: 3-5 year verdict

Good business, poor entry at ₹1,785. On my base case (EPS +9% a year, exit P/E 36x) the price return is only about 1% a year over 3 years and 4% over 5 years. At ₹1,300-1,450 the same base case gives about 8-11% a year over 5 years. Bull case (EPS +14%, P/E 44x) gives about 14% at the IPO price.

Holding fit: Yes, as a core position, if bought at a discount to the IPO price and if you accept regulatory headline risk.

BSE: 3-5 year verdict

Higher expected return, wider outcome range. Base case (EPS +18% a year, exit P/E 38x) gives about 8% over 3 years and 12% over 5 years from ₹3,300. Bear case (EPS +8%, P/E 25x) is about -16% and -7% a year. Growth has already been visible; the question is how much is left.

Holding fit: Yes, as a satellite position with a stop-and-review rule on market share and SEBI actions.

What would make me more positive or more negative

Positive triggers

  • NSE non-transaction revenue rising well beyond 12% of operating revenue
  • Settlement of the SEBI colocation cases with no new actions
  • NSE options revenue growth resuming after FY26's -1.9%
  • BSE reaching double-digit cash share and diversifying beyond options
  • Price correction to the accumulation zones above

Negative triggers

  • Further SEBI limits on weekly expiries, higher lot sizes or higher STT
  • NSE forced into a fee cut to defend share (margin risk)
  • Options premium share for NSE falling below about 60%
  • More broker EPS cuts for BSE, or volume declines beyond the market
  • A major outage or a new governance issue

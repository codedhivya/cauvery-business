IRDAI "Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution" — Sector Impact Analysis

How the draft distribution/commission reforms reshape banks, listed intermediaries and insurers

⚠ Draft consultation paper — not yet notified as regulation Consultation paper released Sept 2026 Feedback window closes 25 Oct 2026 Proposed effective FY28 (glide path)

What IRDAI Has Proposed

IRDAI's consultation paper "Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution" is the most significant proposed overhaul of distribution rules since the 2023 Expenses of Management (EoM) reforms. It re-introduces hard commission caps (reversing the 2023 move to an entity-level EoM ceiling), tightens overall expense limits, and restructures the distributor landscape from eight categories into three: Insurance Distribution Entity (IDE), Insurance Distribution Person (IDP), and Market Infrastructure Institution for Insurance (MII).

All-inclusive caps mean commissions, incentives, rewards, gifts, trips and related-party payments are proposed to be counted together — closing the workaround where insurers paid extra through non-commission channels.

1. Banks — Bancassurance Fee Income Under Pressure

Banks earned over ₹20,000 crore in FY26 from distributing life, health and general insurance — pure fee income with no underwriting risk carried. Bancassurance pays an average commission of ~33% today; the draft proposes compression plus mandatory disclosure of the commission to the customer at point of sale.

Kotak Mahindra Bank, ICICI Bank and SBI already show the highest "other income" (commissions + insurance) as a share of total income among large banks — these are the names with the most to lose in headline fee income, though the actual profit hit is cushioned by a proposed multi-year glide path. Credit-life (single-premium, loan-attached) is the single most exposed product line given the proposed ban on compulsory loan-linked bundling.

2. Listed Intermediaries — Digital Aggregators and Brokers

PB Fintech (Policybazaar) is the main listed pure-play insurance intermediary on Indian exchanges. It holds a Composite Insurance Broker licence and commands ~90% share of the online insurance aggregator segment. The stock has repeatedly moved on IRDAI commission-cap news over the past two years.

Separately, the Insurance Laws (Amendment) Bill — cleared by Cabinet and tabled in the Lok Sabha — proposes raising FDI in insurance to 100% from 74% and formally empowers IRDAI to cap commissions by regulation. That FDI liberalisation is a net positive for the sector even as near-term commission-rule uncertainty weighs on intermediary stocks.

3. Insurers — Where They Benefit

For insurance companies themselves, tighter distribution economics is a double-edged sword: it hurts near-term volumes but improves the long-run quality and profitability of the business they write.

Structural upside for insurers
BenefitWhy it helps insurers
Lower commission leakageMore of the premium rupee stays with the insurer/policyholder instead of being paid out as acquisition cost — supports embedded value and VNB margins over time
Ban on compulsory loan-bundlingEnds forced-attach credit-life sales that generated poor persistency and high lapse/complaint ratios — cleaner, stickier book going forward
Ban on volume-linked staff incentivesReduces mis-selling and consequent claim disputes/reputational cost for insurers
Mandatory commission disclosureRebuilds customer trust in the channel, which over time supports penetration (India's insurance penetration is still low vs global peers)
Reduced dependence on a single bank parentInsurers are pushed toward diversified, multi-channel distribution — reduces single-partner concentration risk (a long-standing IRDAI concern since 2023)
100% FDI proposal (separate Amendment Bill)Easier access to foreign capital for insurers needing to fund the transition to lower-cost distribution models
Simplified 3-tier distributor architecture + lower entry capitalCould widen the pool of small-ticket, rural/semi-urban distributors over time, aiding the "Insurance for all" penetration goal

4. Insurers — Where They Lose / Are At Risk

Industry executives and analysts expect a 2–3 year growth disruption as the new caps bite, with the pain distributed unevenly across insurers.

Downside risks by insurer type
Insurer typeRisk
Bank-promoted life insurers (HDFC Life, SBI Life, ICICI Prudential Life, Kotak Life)Heavy reliance on the parent bank's branch network for distribution — a bancassurance-fee squeeze at the bank flows straight through to new-business volumes for these insurers
Insurers selling credit-life via NBFC/bank loan tie-upsSingle-premium credit-life (commission cut from ~28% to a proposed 2%) is the most exposed product line; compulsory bundling ban removes a large chunk of the current sales funnel outright
Smaller/newer life and general insurersHigher fixed operating costs as a % of premium while scaling up — the tighter EoM ceiling (12.5%→10% for life, 20% for general) is proportionately harder to meet than for large, scaled insurers
Standalone health insurersHealth commission cut from ~40% to a proposed 5% removes a major agent/broker incentive at a time when acquisition costs are already flagged as high and group-health pricing as thin
General insurers (fire/property lines)IRDAI has separately flagged discounting of up to 99% off standard fire-insurance rates — a related governance/underwriting-discipline concern layered on top of the distribution reform
Whole sector, near termAnalysts expect a sharp correction in new-business growth for roughly 2–3 years post-implementation unless the glide path is lengthened; the current proposal already softened once (life EoM glide extended to 5 years) after industry pushback

5. Consultation Status — Draft, Not Yet Regulation

Nothing in this paper is currently binding. IRDAI has issued it as a consultation paper seeking stakeholder feedback — the standard first stage before any regulation is notified. Every figure in the earlier tabs (commission caps, EoM ceilings, the loan-bundling ban) is a proposal, not a rule in force.

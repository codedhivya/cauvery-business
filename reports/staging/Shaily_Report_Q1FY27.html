🏭 Business Profile How Shaily actually makes money
Shaily is a precision polymer contract manufacturer — it does not own consumer brands. Customers bring a product requirement; Shaily contributes tooling, mould design, material science and, increasingly, its own device IP, then manufactures at volume. That single business model is applied to three completely unrelated end-markets, which is why the segments behave nothing like each other.
Healthcare
51% of Q1 FY27 revenue
Drug-delivery devices — pen injectors for insulin and GLP-1 (semaglutide) therapies, plus autoinjectors and an emerging on-body/reusable pipeline. Sold to pharmaceutical companies who fill and brand the device. Revenue is per-unit; Shaily's own IP on several platforms is what lifts the price above a pure moulding fee. Highest margin, longest qualification cycle, hardest to displace.
Consumer
41% of Q1 FY27 revenue
Home furnishings and household products manufactured for large global retailers, plus toys, FMCG packaging and LED lighting applications. Export-led and demand-cyclical — this is the segment that fell 24% on European and US weakness. A new global FMCG project and LED lighting wins were announced in the quarter. Volume business, thinner margin, exposed to a handful of large accounts.
Industrial
8% of Q1 FY27 revenue
Appliance components, consumer-electronics parts and automotive components; semiconductor trays are being added for Q4 FY27. Smallest segment but growing 25% and strategically the diversification hedge — it uses the same moulding asset base with a different customer cycle. Early stage; too small to move group numbers yet.

📈 What the price is assuming
At 86× trailing earnings, essentially none of the current valuation rests on the business as it stands today. The clearest way to see what is embedded is to take the one named brokerage basis available: Motilal Oswal values the stock at 50× FY28E EPS to reach ₹4,074, which implies FY28 EPS of about ₹81 against FY26's ₹36.97 — roughly a 48% annual earnings CAGR for two consecutive years, and still leaves the shares on 50× at the end of it.

That is the debate in one line. The company is delivering the growth so far — three-year PAT CAGR of ~69% is real, disclosed and cash-backed. The question is whether device volumes can compound at that rate for two more years while a segment representing 41% of revenue is guided flat.
Why a trailing multiple mis-reads this business. The sector file's own guidance is that an order-driven manufacturer should be read against forward visibility, not a trailing P/E — and Shaily is running at 50.2% machine utilisation with pen capacity heading to ~75 m units. Earnings can rise substantially without a matching capital cycle. The trailing multiple is therefore both genuinely expensive and structurally overstated; neither half of that sentence should be dropped.

🛡 Moats What is durable, what is only a tailwind — assessed sceptically
The sector file's instruction for this section is to be sceptical: qualification barriers are real moats; policy tailwinds and subsidy access are not. Applied honestly, Shaily has one strong moat, one moderate one, and two things frequently described as moats that are not.
1. Device qualification and regulatory lock-in
A pen injector is qualified as part of a drug-device combination and carries into the customer's regulatory filing. Changing supplier means re-qualification and, in many markets, a regulatory variation — slow, expensive and risky for a pharma customer mid-launch. Six of eight platforms are commercial and approvals were added in Canada and Brazil this quarter. This is the genuine moat, and it is the same mechanism the sector file credits in defence components.
Wide — and widening
2. Owned device IP
Shaily contributes its own platform IP rather than only moulding to a customer's drawing, and signed two further IP-led platform projects this quarter. IP content is what lets it price above $2 while Chinese copycats sit at $1.50–$1.70. Moderate rather than wide, because IP protects the price point but not the volume — the customer still chooses.
Moderate
3. Scale in consumer moulding — not a moat
High-volume home-furnishings moulding is a conversion business. The honest claim is scale plus customer intimacy, and this quarter demonstrated the limit precisely: when the end customer's demand fell, revenue fell 24% and Shaily had no defence. Long relationships are an asset; they are not a barrier to entry.
Not a moat
4. The GLP-1 wave — not a moat
Semaglutide patent expiry in India and Canada is a demand event available to every device maker, and it is the direct cause of the 85% healthcare growth. It should be read as a tailwind with a shape — large now, and eventually normalising as generic competition matures. Confusing a cycle with a franchise is the most likely analytical error on this stock.
Tailwind, not a moat
The test that separates them. Ask what happens if a competitor tries to take the business tomorrow. For a commercial pen platform, they must re-qualify with the regulator through the customer's filing — years. For a home-furnishings component, they quote a lower price — a quarter. Both businesses sit in the same company; only one is defended.

Where the next two quarters will actually be decided. Three things, in order: (1) whether the +25 m line commissions on time in September and converts into Q3 volume; (2) whether gross margin normalises in Q3 as guided, since Q1's 540 bps gross-margin gain was partly mix and the company itself flagged commodity pressure; (3) whether the UK subsidiary stops subtracting from consolidated profit. Consumer is already guided flat, so it is not a swing factor — a further decline there would be, though.

🎯 Verdict A research read of the disclosed numbers — explicitly not investment advice
What Shaily did this quarter, in one paragraph. It converted itself from a consumer-goods moulder that also made medical devices into a medical-device company that also moulds consumer goods — healthcare crossed 51% of revenue for the first time, growing 85% while consumer fell 24%. Revenue hit a record ₹280.7 Cr, EBITDA margin recovered to 29.7%, and RoCE reached 39%. The transition was executed on half-idle machinery with falling leverage, which is why the return ratios improved rather than diluted.
✅ What the numbers clearly support
  • The margin expansion is structural, not cosmetic — it comes from mix, and it survived a tax rate rising to 27% and other income falling to zero.
  • The profit is cash. FY26 converted 98% of operating profit into operating cash flow; debtor days sit at 79.
  • The moat in devices is real and testable — regulatory re-qualification is what protects it, and six of eight platforms are already commercial.
  • Growth is not capex-gated in the near term: at 50.2% utilisation, volume can rise before the next capital cycle bites.
❌ What the numbers do not support
  • “Profit up 46%” — that is standalone. Consolidated PAT rose 17%, and consolidated sits ₹4.5 Cr below standalone.
  • A diversified growth story. Strip healthcare out and the rest of the business shrank ~19% YoY.
  • Order-book style visibility. None is disclosed; the forward case rests on capacity, approvals and management expectation.
  • A free-cash story. ₹226 Cr of FY26 operating cash became ₹41 Cr of free cash; four capex projects are running at once.
The question this quarter did not answer
Semaglutide patent expiry in India and Canada is the direct cause of the 85% healthcare jump. That is a dated event, not a permanent condition — generic competition matures, and Chinese device makers are already quoting $1.50–$1.70 against Shaily's $2-plus. The bull case requires the qualification moat and the move up the price ladder (emergency autoinjectors at $6–$10, on-body at $15–$35) to arrive before GLP-1 device pricing normalises. Nothing in Q1 FY27 settles that; the next four quarters, and specifically whether the flagged “major global pharmaceutical partnership” materialises, will.
On the fundamentals reviewed, Shaily's Q1 FY27 is a high-quality quarter: record revenue, structural margin gain, exceptional returns, clean cash conversion, no accounting flattery, and honest disclosure of the segment that is going backwards. The two things a reader should hold alongside that are the concentration of the growth in one therapy class and the 86× trailing multiple, which together mean the earnings quality and the share-price outcome are separate questions. 🤖 CB Assist Questions this report already answers — jump straight to the reasoning
Every answer below is drawn only from figures disclosed in the sources listed in the footer. No figure appears here that is not in the report above.
Why does one headline say profit rose 46% and another says 17%?
Two different bases. Standalone PAT was ₹52.47 Cr, up 46% from a low ₹35.91 Cr base. Consolidated PAT was ₹48.01 Cr, up 17% from ₹41.0 Cr. Consolidated is below standalone because subsidiaries — principally the UK entity — subtracted ₹4.5 Cr this quarter. Consolidated is the figure used throughout this report.
What actually drove the quarter?
Segment mix. Healthcare grew ₹65.4 Cr (+85%) on semaglutide pen volumes after patent expiry in India and Canada, while consumer lost ₹36.8 Cr (−24%) on weak European and US home-furnishings demand. Healthcare crossed 51% of revenue for the first time. Excluding healthcare, the rest of the business shrank about 19%.
Is the 29.7% EBITDA margin sustainable?
It is mix-driven, so it holds as long as healthcare's share holds. Note it is not a peak — Q2 FY26 was 31.5%. Management flagged commodity and freight pressure on Q1 gross margin and guided normalisation by Q3 FY27. Gross margin rose 540 bps YoY to 56.6%, which is the mechanical source of the EBITDA gain.
How much device capacity is there, and how much is being used?
About 9 million delivery devices shipped in Q1 FY27, of which GLP-1 was roughly 50–60%. FY27 guidance is above 36 million. A new 25 m-unit line is expected operational by end-September 2026, taking pen capacity to roughly 75 million a year, with a further 25 million indicated by September 2027. Overall machine utilisation across the company was 50.2%.
Why did exports fall from 76% to 58% of revenue?
Not because exports collapsed — because domestic grew faster. Indian pharmaceutical customers began buying semaglutide pens domestically after patent expiry, lifting domestic share from 23.9% to 41.9%. It re-bases the currency and receivables profile of the business.
What does the 86× P/E require to be justified?
Using the one named brokerage basis available: Motilal Oswal's ₹4,074 target is 50× FY28E EPS, implying FY28 EPS of about ₹81 against FY26's ₹36.97 — roughly 48% annual earnings growth for two straight years, with the shares still on 50× afterwards. That is what is embedded. CB Research takes no view on whether it is achievable and issues no recommendation.
Where is the real moat, and where is it only a tailwind?
The moat is device qualification — a pen injector sits inside the customer's regulatory filing, so switching supplier means re-qualification measured in years. Owned platform IP is a moderate second. Scale in consumer moulding is not a moat, as the 24% decline demonstrated, and the GLP-1 wave itself is a tailwind available to every device maker, not a franchise.
What was not disclosed that a reader might expect?
Quarter-end net debt and cash, Q1 capex, customer concentration, and any order book or book-to-bill. The company reports on rolling customer programmes rather than a project backlog, so the third is structural rather than an omission — but it does mean this sector's usual forward-visibility test cannot be run on Shaily.

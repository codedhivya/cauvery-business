# Sector: Metals & Mining (India)

Covers integrated and secondary steel producers, aluminium, and diversified natural-resources companies.

**The defining variable is raw-material integration.** Two steel companies with identical selling prices
can have completely different margins depending on whether they mine their own iron ore and coal or buy
them. Integration is what separates a structurally advantaged producer from a price-taker with a
conversion spread, and it is the first thing to establish about any company here.

The second defining fact: **prices are set globally, not by the company.** Steel and aluminium are
commodities priced off international benchmarks. Nobody in this sector has pricing power in the ordinary
sense; the levers are cost, volume and mix.

## 1. Category taxonomy

| Category | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Integrated steel** | Owns iron ore and/or coking coal, converts to finished steel. Best margin through the cycle | Tata Steel, SAIL, JSW Steel (partially integrated) |
| **Secondary / EAF steel** | Buys scrap or sponge iron, melts and rolls. Lower capex, lower margin, more price-exposed | Shyam Metallics, Jindal Steel & Power |
| **Aluminium — integrated** | Bauxite → alumina → aluminium, often with captive power | NALCO, Hindalco (with Novelis downstream) |
| **Diversified resources** | Multiple metals and often oil & gas under one holding | Vedanta |

Compare within a category. An integrated producer's 20% EBITDA margin and a secondary producer's 10% are
not the same achievement — the first reflects owned ore, the second a conversion spread.

## 2. How companies in this sector make money

Revenue is **volume × realisation**, and realisation follows global benchmark prices the company does not
set. So the entire competitive question is cost per tonne, and cost is dominated by raw material.

**For steel**, the inputs are iron ore and coking coal. A producer with captive mines pays extraction
cost; one without pays the market price, which can double in a tight year. That gap is the single largest
driver of margin dispersion across Indian steel companies, and it widens exactly when input prices spike
— integrated producers earn most when unintegrated ones suffer most.

India is short of coking coal and imports most of it, so **coking coal is the recurring margin shock**;
iron ore is more domestically available, which is why iron-ore integration is the more common advantage.

**For aluminium**, the chain runs bauxite → alumina → aluminium metal, and it is enormously
power-intensive — power is typically the largest single cost after alumina, which is why captive power
plants matter as much as captive mines. Realisation tracks the **LME** price plus a regional premium.

**Downstream and value-added mix** is the other lever. Auto-grade, coated, and specialty products earn
better and more stable realisations than commodity long products, so a rising value-added share
structurally lifts margin independent of price cycles.

**Working the cycle**: this sector is heavily capital-intensive and historically over-leverages at the
top of a cycle. Net debt and the debt-to-EBITDA position at a cyclical peak is the standard warning
signal — EBITDA is inflated at the peak, so leverage looks deceptively comfortable exactly when it is
most dangerous.

### The analogy

A converter. It buys ore, coal and power, sells metal, and lives on the gap. Because the output price is set globally and much of the cost is fixed, a small move in the spread swings profit enormously — which is why per-tonne economics matter more than revenue.

## 3. Metric definitions

- **Sales volume (tonnes)** — the volume driver, usually disclosed monthly or quarterly by segment.
- **Realisation per tonne (₹/t)** = revenue ÷ volume. Follows benchmark prices; the company's control is
  limited to mix and geography.
- **EBITDA per tonne (₹/t)** — **the sector's headline profitability metric**, and far more comparable
  across companies than EBITDA margin because it neutralises differences in realisation. When steel
  prices fall, margin % and EBITDA/t both fall, but EBITDA/t is what management guides on and what peers
  are ranked by.
- **Captive integration (%)** — the share of iron ore, coking coal or bauxite sourced from own mines.
  **The most important structural disclosure in this sector.** State it as a percentage per input.
- **Conversion cost / cost of production per tonne** — what the company controls.
- **Capacity and capacity utilisation (%)** — installed tonnage and how hard it runs.
- **LME price and regional premium** — the aluminium realisation driver.
- **Coking coal and iron ore prices** — the steel cost drivers; state the benchmark used.
- **Value-added / downstream share (%)** — auto-grade, coated, specialty as a share of volume.
- **Net debt and net debt / EBITDA** — the cycle-risk metric. Read against mid-cycle EBITDA, not peak.
- **Export share** — diversification, but also exposure to trade policy.

## 4. Benchmarks — what good looks like

Indicative and cycle-dependent; these move with commodity prices, so verify against the current period
and the company's own history rather than treating them as fixed.

| Metric | Healthy |
|---|---|
| EBITDA margin — integrated steel | 18–25% at mid-cycle |
| EBITDA margin — secondary/EAF steel | 8–14% |
| EBITDA margin — integrated aluminium | 20–28% |
| EBITDA per tonne — steel | the peer-relative measure; compare against the domestic peer set for the same quarter |
| Capacity utilisation | >85% |
| Captive iron ore | higher is better; full integration is a genuine structural advantage |
| Net debt / EBITDA | <2.5× at mid-cycle. **Below 2× at a cyclical peak, since peak EBITDA flatters the ratio** |
| Value-added share | rising; reduces cyclicality |

**What to watch, by category:**

- **Integrated steel** — What is captive coverage for ore and coal separately? Is EBITDA/tonne holding
  against peers? Is the value-added mix rising? Where is net debt against mid-cycle EBITDA, not current?
- **Secondary steel** — Is the conversion spread holding? Scrap and sponge-iron cost direction? Utilisation?
- **Aluminium** — Where is LME, and what regional premium is realised? Is captive power and bauxite
  coverage intact? Is downstream (Novelis-type) value-add growing?
- **Diversified resources** — Which segment is actually driving the result? Holding-company structures can
  mask a weak division with a strong one; insist on the segment split.

### CBAM — a carbon tariff, and why it lands unevenly

**CBAM (Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism) is the EU's carbon tariff on imports** into the bloc, aimed
at carbon-intensive goods including steel, cement and aluminium. An exporter selling into the EU pays for
the **carbon embedded in the product** where its home country has no equivalent carbon price.

Two consequences a reader needs:

- **It is an exporter's problem, sized by carbon intensity, not by volume.** A blast-furnace route
  carries far more embedded carbon than an electric-arc route running on scrap, so **two producers
  shipping identical tonnage into the EU face very different bills**. State EU export share *and* the
  production route; either alone misleads.
- **A producer already inside the EU is not subject to CBAM** — it faces the EU's own emissions trading
  scheme instead. That is not relief: it is the same carbon cost arriving by a different door, and
  applied to assets that are expensive to decarbonise. **An Indian producer with European operations is
  exposed on both sides** and should be read that way.

### What goes wrong, and the tell

**A cost base that cannot move as fast as the price.** The tells: realisation falling while cost per tonne holds, which compresses the spread from both ends; inventory built at high prices being sold into a falling market; captive raw-material share dropping; and expansion capex committed at the top of a cycle, which is how balance sheets in this sector historically break.

## 5. Regulatory and policy quick reference

Policy moves prices here more directly than in most sectors.

| Area | Body / issue |
|---|---|
| Import protection | **Safeguard duty and anti-dumping** on steel — directly supports domestic realisations |
| Export policy | export duty on iron ore and steel; changes have historically been abrupt |
| Mining | Ministry of Mines; mineral auctions, mining leases, and royalty rates |
| Trade agreements | **FTAs** affect import competition — an existing report tracks FTA impact explicitly |
| Environmental | pollution-control norms, carbon-related costs, and the coming EU CBAM for exporters |
| Industry data | **JPC (Joint Plant Committee)** for steel production and consumption; **Ministry of Steel** |

Safeguard duty and FTA changes are the two events that most reliably move Indian steel margins, and both
are policy decisions rather than market outcomes.

## 6. Per-mode specifics

### Headline KPIs by category (`dashboard`)

| Category | KPIs |
|---|---|
| Integrated steel | Revenue, sales volume, realisation/t, **EBITDA/t**, EBITDA margin, PAT, net debt, captive % |
| Secondary steel | Revenue, sales volume, realisation/t, EBITDA/t, EBITDA margin, PAT, utilisation |
| Aluminium | Revenue, production volume, LME realisation, EBITDA/t, EBITDA margin, PAT, net debt, captive power % |
| Diversified resources | Revenue and EBITDA **by segment**, net debt, PAT, segment contribution |

### Table columns by category (`financials`)

- **All** — Period, Sales volume (t), Revenue, YoY, Realisation/t, **EBITDA, EBITDA/t**, EBITDA%, PAT, Net debt
- **Raw materials table** — recurs as an explicit section in existing reports: input, price movement,
  captive coverage %, cost per tonne impact. This is where the sector's margin story actually lives.
- **Debt & cash flow table** — net debt, net debt/EBITDA, operating cash flow, capex. Mandatory for a
  capital-intensive cyclical.
- **Segment and geography table** — for diversified companies, non-negotiable.

### Chart reference lines (`charts`)

| Metric | Line | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Capacity utilisation | 85% | "Efficient utilisation" |
| Net debt / EBITDA | 2.5× | "Mid-cycle comfort" — note that peak EBITDA understates this |
| EBITDA per tonne | peer median for the period | "Peer median" — no absolute standard exists; it moves with the cycle |
| LME / benchmark price | the period's average | label the benchmark and period explicitly |

**Prefer EBITDA per tonne over EBITDA margin when charting peers** — margin moves with realisation, so a
margin chart in a falling-price quarter shows the price, not the performance.

### Profile coverage by category (`business-profile`)

- **Steel** — capacity by plant and route (blast furnace vs EAF), **captive mine coverage for ore and
  coal stated separately**, product mix (flat/long/value-added), geographic sales mix, export share,
  expansion pipeline with commissioning timelines.
- **Aluminium** — bauxite, alumina and smelting capacity, captive power, downstream/value-added presence,
  LME exposure and hedging policy.
- **Diversified** — segment structure, contribution by segment, any restructuring or demerger underway.

Existing reports carry **USPs** and **Concentration Risks** sections — cover both, grounded in numbers.

### Moat candidates by category (`moats`)

- **Captive raw material** — the genuine moat in this sector. Owned iron ore or coking coal is a
  permanent cost advantage a competitor cannot buy, since mining leases are finite and auctioned. Cite
  coverage percentages.
- **Captive power** — for aluminium, where power is a dominant cost, this is equally structural.
- **Scale and location** — proximity to ore or to consuming markets reduces freight, which is material
  on a low-value-per-tonne product.
- **Downstream / value-added capability** — auto-grade and specialty steel qualification takes years and
  reduces cyclicality.
- **Be sceptical** of low-cost-producer claims without the cost-per-tonne number, and of "capacity
  expansion" as a moat — adding tonnes in a commodity is not an advantage unless the cost position is
  better than the incumbent's.

### Valuation (`valuation`)

Primary multiple: **EV/EBITDA** — the right basis for a leveraged, capital-intensive cyclical, since P/E
is distorted by capital structure and by depreciation policy. **EV per tonne of capacity** is the
standard cross-check. **P/B** is meaningful at cyclical troughs when earnings are negative or negligible.

**The cyclical trap is acute here and must be stated.** A trailing P/E at a price peak looks cheap and at
a trough looks expensive — precisely backwards. Read multiples against **mid-cycle EBITDA** where
possible and say which basis is used; a metals valuation without a stated cycle position is close to
meaningless.

### CB Rating substitutions (`cb-rating`)

Capital-intensive cyclical: leverage and cash flow get an explicit component, following the
`power-energy` precedent.

| Component | Weight | Metals substitution |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | 25% | Sales volume growth and capacity addition — **not revenue growth**, which mostly reflects commodity prices the company does not control |
| Profitability | 25% | **EBITDA per tonne against the peer median**, plus margin direction — the price-neutral comparison |
| PAT Quality | 15% | PAT adjusted for one-offs, forex and exceptional items, which are frequent here |
| Debt & Cash Flow | 20% | Net debt/EBITDA **against mid-cycle EBITDA**, operating cash conversion, capex funding |
| Cost Position & Outlook | 15% | **Captive integration %**, conversion cost, value-added mix, and price/policy outlook |

Total 100%. Growth deliberately uses **volume, not revenue** — a steel company whose revenue rose 30%
because steel prices rose 30% has not grown, and revenue-based scoring would reward the commodity cycle
rather than the company.

### Extra sections (`quarterly-report`)

- **Raw Materials** — input prices, captive coverage, cost per tonne. Recurs in existing reports and is
  the heart of this sector.
- **Volumes** — production and sales by product and segment.
- **Debt & Cash Flow** — the capital-intensive standard section.
- **Segments & Geography** — recurs in existing reports.
- **Import Policy / Trade** — safeguard duty, anti-dumping, FTA impact. Existing reports track these
  explicitly and they move realisations directly.
- **USPs** and **Concentration Risks** — both appear in existing reports.

### Event transmission map (`event-impact`, `risks-outlook`)

| Event | Reaches results via | Exposure basis to cite |
|---|---|---|
| **Steel price rise** | own realisation up, **and the customer industry's margin down** | EU export share and production route for CBAM; for the read-across, the downstream sector's ability to pass it on |
| Coking coal price move | cost per tonne, margin | **captive coal coverage %**; imported share |
| Iron ore price move | cost per tonne | captive ore coverage % |
| LME aluminium price move | realisation | production volume, hedge position |
| Safeguard duty imposed or lifted | domestic realisation | domestic vs export sales mix |
| Anti-dumping ruling | import competition, pricing | affected product share |
| FTA signed or revised | import competition | exposure to the counterparty's exports |
| Export duty change | export realisation and volumes | export share of sales |
| Mining lease auction or expiry | captive coverage, future cost | leases due for renewal |
| Power tariff change | aluminium cost | captive power share |
| Auto or construction demand shift | volumes and mix | end-use sales split |
| EU CBAM implementation | export competitiveness | EU export share, carbon intensity |

## 7. Where to look (sourcing)

**Tier 1** — quarterly investor presentations, which disclose sales volume, realisation per tonne,
EBITDA per tonne, captive coverage and segment splits. **Many steel companies publish monthly production
and sales volumes** to the exchanges, so the quarter's volume direction is known in advance. Earnings
calls carry the guidance on realisation and cost that matters most.

**Tier 2 — sector authorities**: **JPC (Joint Plant Committee)** for steel production, consumption and
import/export data — the right denominator for market-share claims; **Ministry of Steel** and
**Ministry of Mines**; **LME** for aluminium benchmark pricing; **DGTR** for anti-dumping and safeguard
proceedings, which are the sector's most consequential policy events.

Benchmark commodity prices should be cited with the benchmark named and the period stated — "steel
prices rose" is not a sourced claim.

## 10. Mode applicability

All 15 modes apply. Notes:

- **`segments`** — product mix (flat/long/value-added), geography, and for diversified companies the
  segment split, which is essential rather than optional.
- **`charts`** — prefer **EBITDA per tonne** over EBITDA margin for peer comparison; margin charts in a
  price-moving quarter show the commodity, not the company.
- **`valuation`** — EV/EBITDA primary, EV per tonne of capacity as cross-check, P/B at troughs.
  **Always state the cycle position**; a trailing multiple alone is actively misleading here.
- **`cb-rating`** — five components with explicit Debt & Cash Flow and Cost Position. Growth is scored on
  **volume, not revenue**, since revenue mostly reflects commodity prices.
- **`event-impact`** — one of the most policy-sensitive sectors: safeguard duty, anti-dumping, FTAs and
  export duty all move realisations directly.
- **Cross-sector work** — all universal metrics apply cleanly: Revenue, EBITDA%, PAT, Mkt Cap, P/E,
  EV/EBITDA and Net Debt are all meaningful, and this sector participates fully. **Flag cycle position
  when it appears in a cross-sector table** — a metals company at a cyclical peak will screen as cheap on
  trailing P/E against structurally stable sectors, and that comparison misleads. EBITDA per tonne,
  realisation, captive coverage and LME exposure are sector-specific and stay in their own section.

# Sector: Capital Goods & Engineering (India)

Covers order-driven manufacturers and engineering companies — defence, railways, electronics
manufacturing services (EMS), cables and wires, power transmission & distribution equipment, and
industrial equipment.

**The defining characteristic: revenue is contracted before it is earned.** These companies sell against
an order book, and the analytical question is almost always about conversion — how much work is booked,
how fast it turns into revenue, and at what margin. Nearly every metric here serves that question.

## 1. Category taxonomy

| Category | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Defence** | Platforms, systems and components for the armed forces; long cycles, single dominant customer | Bharat Electronics, HAL, Mazagon Dock, GRSE, Data Patterns, Paras Defence |
| **Railways** | Rolling stock, track, signalling, and railway financing | RVNL, IRFC, IRCON |
| **EMS — box-build** | High-volume consumer assembly. The customer owns the design; the manufacturer earns a conversion fee. Enormous revenue, thin margin, working-capital heavy | Dixon |
| **EMS — design-led / ODM** | Contributes design and owns some IP. ODM work earns materially better margin than pure contract assembly | Syrma SGS, Amber |
| **EMS — industrial / semiconductor** | Industrial, aerospace, medical and semiconductor packaging (OSAT). Longer qualification cycles, fewer competitors, better pricing — but heavy capex | Kaynes |
| **Cables & Wires** | Wires, cables, and increasingly FMEG products | Polycab, Bajel |
| **Power T&D Equipment** | Transformers, switchgear, grid equipment — sells *to* the power sector, not part of it | Hitachi Energy India, ABB India, Siemens Energy India, Voltamp, CG Power |
| **Industrial Equipment** | Pumps, cryogenic and process equipment, specialised engineering | MTAR Technologies, INOX India, Shakti Pumps, Shaily Engineering, VA Tech Wabag (water) |

**Boundary note**: companies that *make equipment for* the power sector belong here; companies that
*generate or sell* power belong in `power-energy.md`. A transformer maker is an order-book business with
a book-to-bill ratio; a generator is a capacity-and-tariff business with a PLF. They are analysed
differently despite both being "power" companies.

## 2. How companies in this sector make money

An order is won, executed over months or years, and recognised as revenue as it is delivered. Profit
depends on three things: **winning enough work**, **executing it on schedule**, and **having priced the
input costs correctly at bid time**.

That last point is what makes this sector distinctive. A fixed-price order won two years ago is executed
at today's steel, copper and component prices. When inputs rise faster than expected, margin compresses
on work already booked and there is often no recourse. This is why order-book growth alone is a weak
signal — an order book won at bad prices is a liability, not an asset.

The four axes:

| Axis | Question | Core metrics |
|---|---|---|
| **Order intake** | Is new work being won? | Order inflow, book-to-bill |
| **Visibility** | How much future revenue is contracted? | Order book, order book / revenue |
| **Execution** | Is booked work converting to revenue? | Execution rate, revenue growth vs order book growth |
| **Cash** | Is recognised profit becoming cash? | Debtor days, working capital, receivables from government customers |

### The analogy

A business that sells a promise and delivers it years later. Revenue is yesterday's order book being executed, so today's order intake tells you about the future and today's revenue tells you about the past. Read both, and never one for the other.

## 3. Metric definitions

- **Order book (or backlog)** — contracted work not yet executed. The forward revenue visibility.
- **Order inflow** — new orders won in the period. **The leading indicator**; order book is the lagging
  stock, inflow is the flow.
- **Book-to-bill ratio** = order inflow ÷ revenue for the period. Above 1.0 means the order book is
  growing; below 1.0 means the company is executing faster than it is winning. **The single most useful
  ratio in this sector**, because it captures direction in one number.
- **Order book / revenue (× or months)** — years of revenue visibility. Interpret against the category's
  natural cycle: three years is normal for defence, alarming for EMS.
- **Execution rate** — how much of the opening order book converted to revenue. Slipping execution shows
  up as an order book that grows while revenue does not, which looks like success and isn't.
- **EBITDA margin** — for fixed-price contracts this is set at bid time; margin compression usually means
  input costs moved after the bid, not that the company suddenly ran the business worse.
- **Debtor days / receivables** — critical here. Government and PSU customers pay slowly, and a company
  can report strong profit while cash sits in receivables for quarters.
- **Working capital / revenue** — the structural cash need of the business model.
- **Capacity utilisation** — for manufacturing-led players (cables, EMS).
- **Customer concentration** — an EMS business may derive most revenue from two or three brands. State
  the share; it is the sector's most common single-point risk.
- **PLI benefit** — production-linked incentive accruals materially flatter EMS and electronics margins.
  Disclose whether reported margin is with or without PLI, since the scheme has an end date.
- **Project IRR** — where a company builds and operates (BOO/BOOT contracts rather than pure supply), the
  return underwritten at bid. Distinguish supply contracts from build-own-operate ones: the second ties up
  capital for years and should not be read on the same margin basis.
- **Realisation per tonne / per km** — for volume-linked businesses such as cables, where copper and
  aluminium tonnage drives both input cost and revenue.

## 4. Benchmarks — what good looks like

Indicative — read against the company's own cycle and its category.

| Metric | Healthy |
|---|---|
| Book-to-bill | >1.0; sustained >1.2 is strong |
| Order book / revenue | 2–3× for defence and railways; ~1× or less is normal for EMS |
| EBITDA margin (defence, precision) | 15–25% |
| **EBITDA margin (EMS — box-build)** | **2–4%** — structurally thin; scale and working capital are the levers |
| **EBITDA margin (EMS — design-led / ODM)** | **9–13%** — design contribution and IP lift the band |
| **EBITDA margin (EMS — industrial / semiconductor)** | **14–18%** — qualification barriers and fewer competitors |
| EBITDA margin (cables & wires) | 11–14% |
| EBITDA margin (power T&D equipment) | 12–18% |
| Debtor days | <90 comfortable; >120 warrants explanation, especially with PSU customers |
| Net debt / EBITDA | <2× |
| Customer concentration (EMS) | top client <30% of revenue is comfortable |

**What to watch, by category:**

- **Defence** — Is order inflow keeping pace with execution? Is indigenisation content rising (higher
  margin)? Are deliveries slipping on supply-chain constraints? How concentrated is the customer?
- **Railways** — Is the government capex cycle supporting inflow? Are receivables from PSU customers
  lengthening? Is the order mix shifting toward higher-margin systems?
- **EMS** — **Establish the ladder position before judging the margin.** A box-build assembler earning 3%
  is performing normally; an industrial EMS earning 3% would be in serious trouble. The comparable
  question is not the level but the **direction within the band**, and whether the mix is moving up the
  ladder — box-build → design-led/ODM → industrial. Then: is customer concentration falling? What is
  PLI's share of reported profit, and when does it end?
- **Cables & Wires** — Is copper/aluminium cost being passed through? Is the FMEG segment scaling? Is
  distribution reach expanding?
- **Power T&D Equipment** — Is grid capex driving inflow? Is pricing discipline holding, or is the order
  book being bought with low bids?
- **Industrial Equipment** — Is exports/replacement mix rising? Is the aftermarket (higher margin) growing?

### How an order was won decides what it is worth

An order book is not one thing. For businesses serving government and public-sector customers, the
**award mechanism** determines both the margin and the certainty of the work:

- **Nomination / directly assigned orders** — allocated by the ministry or agency without a competitive
  process, typically to a public-sector entity. **Lower margin, higher certainty**: the work is close to
  guaranteed, and priced accordingly.
- **Competitively bid orders** — won against other bidders. **Potentially higher margin, genuinely more
  risk**, on execution and on the price that had to be bid to win.

**Ask for the split.** Two companies with identical order books have different earnings quality if one is
mostly nominated and the other mostly bid, and a shift in the mix changes forward margin without any
change in order intake. A move away from nomination toward competitive bidding is a **structural margin
event** and is usually announced long before it shows in results.

### What goes wrong, and the tell

**An order book that does not convert.** Orders are announced; revenue is executed. The tells: book-to-bill strong while revenue growth lags, meaning execution or clearances are stuck; receivables lengthening, which for government-facing businesses is the normal failure; provisioning on old orders; and margin falling on fixed-price contracts as input costs move.

## 5. Regulatory and policy quick reference

No single regulator; policy is the demand driver:

| Area | Body / scheme |
|---|---|
| Defence procurement, indigenisation lists | Ministry of Defence — DAP, positive indigenisation lists |
| Railway capex | Indian Railways / Ministry of Railways budget allocations |
| Electronics manufacturing | **PLI schemes** (MeitY) — check scheme tenure and accrual treatment |
| Public procurement preference | Make in India / local-content rules |
| Quality control | BIS standards, applicable QCOs |

Government capex allocation in the Union Budget is a genuine demand signal for defence, railways and
grid equipment — worth tracking as a sector event, not just company news.

### Table columns by category (`financials`)

- **All categories** — a working-capital table: Debtor days, Inventory days, Working capital % of

- **EMS** — add customer concentration and PLI accrual as explicit rows.

### Chart reference lines (`charts`)

| Metric | Line | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Book-to-bill | 1.0 | "Order book flat" — above means growing, below means shrinking |
| Debtor days | 90 | "Comfortable receivables" |
| Net debt / EBITDA | 2.0× | "Comfortable leverage" |
| Capacity utilisation | 85% | "Efficient utilisation" |

All are industry norms, not regulatory thresholds — label them as such.

**Charting EMS margins needs three reference lines, not one.** Plotting Dixon, Syrma and Kaynes against a
single band makes the box-build player look broken and the industrial player look exceptional, when both
are performing normally for their rung. Draw the ladder bands — ~3% box-build, ~11% design-led,
~16% industrial — and label each, or plot the categories on separate charts.

### Profile coverage by category (`business-profile`)

Cover for every company: **business model and verticals**, the **order book composition** (by customer
type, segment and geography), **key JVs, acquisitions and technology tie-ups** — this sector grows
through partnerships far more than most, and EMS in particular is JV-heavy — and **governance
considerations**, which recur as an explicit section in these reports.

- **Defence** — platform/system/component position, indigenisation content, customer profile, export approvals.
- **EMS** — verticals served (consumer, auto, industrial, medical), customer names and concentration,
  backward integration progress, JV structure and partner.
- **Cables** — B2B vs retail mix, distribution reach, FMEG expansion, brand.
- **Power T&D** — parent/technology relationship for MNC-owned entities, product range, grid exposure.

### Moat candidates by category (`moats`)

- **Defence** — qualification and approval barriers. A component qualified on a defence platform is
  extremely hard to displace, because requalification is slow and risky for the customer. Cite the
  platforms qualified on.
- **Railways** — approved-vendor status and long qualification cycles.
- **EMS** — depends entirely on the rung. **Box-build assembly has a thin moat**: it is commoditised, and
  scale plus customer intimacy is the honest claim. **Industrial and semiconductor EMS has a real one** —
  qualification cycles for aerospace, medical and OSAT work run years, and a qualified supplier is
  costly to displace. Design-led/ODM sits between: owned IP and design contribution create some lock-in.
  Be sceptical of "PLI advantage" as a moat — it is a time-limited subsidy available to competitors too,
  and it ends.
- **Cables** — brand plus distribution reach in a market where electricians and contractors drive choice;
  hard to build quickly.
- **Power T&D** — MNC parent technology access, installed base driving service and replacement revenue.
- **Industrial Equipment** — precision capability, customer-embedded design, aftermarket annuity.

### Valuation (`valuation`)

Primary multiple: **P/E**, with **EV/EBITDA** as the cross-check where leverage differs. Because the
business is order-driven, an **order book / market cap** view and P/E against forward visibility are more
informative than a trailing multiple alone. Rerating in this sector typically follows order inflow, not
reported earnings, and multiples can look expensive precisely when visibility is strongest — say so
rather than calling it expensive without that context.

### CB Rating substitutions (`cb-rating`)

Order-driven and working-capital-heavy, so both the growth and quality components are substituted.

| Component | Weight | Capital-goods substitution |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | 30% | **Order inflow and book-to-bill**, alongside revenue growth — revenue growth without order inflow is executing the backlog down |
| Profitability | 25% | EBITDA margin **direction**, judged against the company's own category band — for EMS, its rung on the ladder (box-build 2–4%, design-led 9–13%, industrial 14–18%). Scoring raw margin across rungs measures positioning, not performance |
| PAT Quality | 25% | **Operating cash conversion and debtor days** — profit stuck in PSU receivables is not profit yet |
| Forward Outlook | 20% | Order book / revenue visibility, execution commentary, policy and capex pipeline |

Total 100%.

### Extra sections (`quarterly-report`)

- **Order Book & Execution** — inflow, book, book-to-bill, composition, execution commentary. The
  centrepiece section for this sector.
- **Working Capital** — debtor days, inventory, cash conversion. Frequently the difference between a good
  P&L and a good business.
- **Management & Board / Governance Considerations** — recurs as an explicit section in these reports.
- **Key JVs & Acquisitions** — partnerships and technology tie-ups, especially for EMS.
- **FY Outlook** — guidance on inflow, execution and margin.

### Event transmission map (`event-impact`, `risks-outlook`)

| Event | Reaches results via | Exposure basis to cite |
|---|---|---|
| Defence budget allocation change | order inflow pipeline | defence share of order book |
| Positive indigenisation list addition | addressable market, margin | products on the list |
| Railway capex allocation | order inflow | railway share of order book |
| PLI scheme change or expiry | reported margin | PLI accrual as % of EBITDA |
| Copper / aluminium / steel price move | fixed-price contract margin | input share of cost, order book priced pre-move |
| Import duty or QCO on components | input cost, competitive position | imported input share |
| Large order win or cancellation | visibility, revenue phasing | order value vs annual revenue |
| Government payment delays | working capital, cash | PSU receivables share |
| Grid / transmission capex announcement | T&D equipment inflow | grid segment share |

## 7. Where to look (sourcing)

**Tier 1** — quarterly investor presentations and earnings calls. **Order inflow, order book and
book-to-bill are usually disclosed only in the presentation or on the call**, not in the press release,
so the presentation is essential here. Management commentary on execution and margin guidance carries
more signal in this sector than in most, because it is forward-looking against contracted work.

**Tier 2 — sector authorities**: **Ministry of Defence** (DAP, indigenisation lists, contract
announcements), **Ministry of Railways** (capex allocations, tender awards), **MeitY** (PLI scheme
notifications and disbursement data), **BIS** (standards and QCOs), and Union Budget capital-expenditure
allocations, which are the demand signal for defence, railways and grid.

Large orders are typically announced as exchange filings on award — those filings are the authoritative
record of order value and execution timeline.

## 10. Mode applicability

All 15 modes apply. Notes:

- **`segments`** — order book composition by customer, segment and geography is the meaningful cut, more
  so than revenue split.
- **`risks-outlook`** — unusually important. Execution slippage, input-cost movement on fixed-price
  contracts, customer concentration and PSU receivables are live risks in most quarters.
- **`valuation`** — P/E primary, but read against order-book visibility; a trailing multiple alone
  mis-prices an order-driven business.
- **`cb-rating`** — substitutes order inflow/book-to-bill into Growth and cash conversion into PAT
  Quality; see the table above.
- **`moats`** — be sceptical here. Qualification barriers in defence are real moats; PLI benefits and
  "Make in India tailwinds" are not, since they are available to competitors and time-limited.
- **Cross-sector work** — all universal metrics apply cleanly: Revenue, EBITDA%, PAT, Mkt Cap, P/E,
  EV/EBITDA and Net Debt are all meaningful. Order book, order inflow and book-to-bill are
  sector-specific and stay out of cross-sector tables.
